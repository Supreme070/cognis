▪ The Opportunity

How big is Africa’s AI opportunity?

Mastercard projects that Africa’s AI market will reach $16.53 billion by 2030, growing by an average of 27.42% each year, in its 2025 AI in Africa analysis. Wider estimates are larger: GSMA says AI could add up to $2.9 trillion to Africa’s economy by 2030, the African Development Bank projects up to $1 trillion in extra economic output by 2035, and the IMF estimates that faster adoption could raise Africa’s economy by about 4% over the next decade.
The mobile foundation this runs on is already substantial: mobile technologies contributed $240 billion to Africa’s economy in 2025 — 7.8% of GDP — and the GSMA forecasts $290 billion by 2030, driven by 4G, 5G, and AI (GSMA Mobile Economy Africa, 2026).
▪ Real Adoption

How widely are African enterprises actually adopting AI?

The honest answer: far less than the headlines suggest, and far more than the pessimists claim. Microsoft’s 2025 AI Diffusion Report found that no African country has reached 20% population-level AI adoption. Yet among Sub-Saharan African organisations surveyed in the CIO100 report (2025), adoption runs at 55% — and more than 75% of young Africans already use AI tools weekly.
The global benchmark makes the gap concrete: McKinsey’s State of AI 2025 survey found 88% of organisations worldwide now use AI in at least one business function, up from 78% a year earlier. African enterprise adoption is real and accelerating — but it is concentrated in large organisations, in fintech and payments, and in Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, and Egypt.
▪ The Gaps

What is holding African enterprise AI back?

Research repeatedly points to five gaps: reliable electricity, data centres, internet access, skills and support for African languages. In our client work, we also see AI tools launched without clear rules or audit evidence, and employees given tools they have not been trained to use.
The problem is not simply starting an AI project; it is turning one into a useful part of the business. MIT Media Lab reported that 95% of business generative-AI trials showed no measurable profit impact, while S&P Global found that 42% of companies abandoned most initiatives before everyday use. Clear goals, ownership, testing and staff preparation make the difference.
▪ What Works

What separates the enterprises whose AI works?

Across Cognis Group’s own work in financial services, insurance and operations, carefully chosen systems have cut the time needed to sort support requests by 40–70%, cut document-handling time by 60–90% and reduced forecasting errors by 15–30%. These results depend on measuring the old process first, setting clear rules before the build and training the people whose work will change.
“African organisations can move directly to better ways of working, but the opportunity only pays off when clear responsibility and safety checks are built in.” — Supreme Oyewumi, Founder & AI Engineer, Cognis Group.
▪ The Playbook

What should African enterprise leaders do in 2026?

Take five steps in order: measure how two or three promising processes work today; agree what improvement will count as success; follow the laws, standards and sector rules that apply; train people for the work that will actually change; then put one well-controlled system into everyday use before funding ten more trials.
